AI Dependence & the Trajectory of Data-Driven Business Decisions

Research questionHow has the dependency on AI changed the trajectory for data-driven business decisions, and what role does it play in shaping how organizations rely on it?

By Sawyer R. Walker · Data current as of November 2026 · Work in progress

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has quickly moved from an experiment to an everyday part of how organizations analyze data and make decisions. This project dashboard examines how growing dependence on AI is changing the direction of data-driven decision making, and who benefits or faces challenges during this new age of technology as a result. This dashboard highlights four major areas of research regarding the job market and its effects on hiring, the use of AI in businesses and corporations, financial outcomes for these businesses, and the overall data literacy for the general population in the new age of AI. Each area is divided into three visualizations that present positive and negative outcomes from government, academic, and industry sources, allowing them to be compared side by side so that the findings reflect the full outcome of this research while eliminating bias.

Overview of the Four Research Areas

This research is organized into four major areas: (1) the job market and its effects on hiring, (2) the use of AI in businesses and corporations, (3) financial outcomes for these businesses, and (4) data literacy in the new age of AI. Each area weighs positive and negative evidence side by side. Select an area to see its full breakdown.

1. Job market & hiring

Overall employment is stable, but entry-level hiring in AI-exposed jobs is falling.

2. Business use of AI

Adoption keeps rising, though most firms use AI in only a few functions.

3. Financial outcomes

Productivity gains are real, but enterprise-wide profit impact is still rare.

4. Data & AI literacy

Use of AI is outpacing the training and skills needed to judge its output.

The Rise of AI

The release of ChatGPT in November 2022 marked a turning point, bringing generative AI out of research labs and into everyday life and work, and it was widely reported as one of the fastest-adopted consumer technologies in history. In 2023, the race accelerated as OpenAI released GPT-4, companies such as Google, Anthropic and Meta launched competing models, and Microsoft began building AI "copilots" directly into workplace software. By 2024, AI tools had become multimodal, able to work with text, images and audio, and businesses moved from testing chatbots to applying AI in areas like customer service, marketing and data analysis. In 2025, the focus shifted toward AI "agents" that can complete multi-step tasks on their own, and 88% of organizations reported using AI in at least one business function. Today, AI is no longer an experiment but part of how organizations analyze data and make decisions, setting the tone for the growing dependence this dashboard examines.

Extended Analysis

1. What the Job Market May Look Like in the Future

Forecasts point to more jobs overall, but different ones: roles tied to AI and data grow, while routine office and service roles shrink.

Projected employment change by occupation, 2024–2034

Percent change in jobs. Hover over a bar to see the number of jobs.

GrowthDeclineAll jobs (benchmark)

What you're looking at: Each bar is one occupation. Bars to the right of the center line are expected to grow, and bars to the left are expected to shrink. The gray bar is the average for all U.S. jobs (+3.1%), so anything longer than it is growing faster than the economy. The fastest-growing roles, like data scientists, build or use AI, while the shrinking roles involve routine tasks such as answering customer questions or processing paperwork, which AI tools can now handle.

Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment Projections 2024–34.

Key takeaway: The future job market is less about fewer jobs and more about different jobs. Workers with data and AI skills are positioned to benefit, while routine office and service roles face the most pressure to retrain.

2. Affected Demographics: Who Bears the Costs of AI in the Economy

AI's effects do not fall evenly. Gender and race shape who is exposed to automation and who holds the jobs AI is creating.

Women’s share of workers

Total workforce vs. AI-vulnerable jobs

What you're looking at: Women hold just under half of all U.S. jobs but more than 4 in 5 of the jobs most vulnerable to AI, such as administrative and clerical work. Women of color alone hold more than 30% of the 15 most vulnerable jobs.

Source: National Partnership for Women & Families (2026).

Key takeaway: The costs of AI in the economy fall unevenly. Women, and especially women of color, hold most of the jobs AI is most likely to change, so access to training and fair hiring will shape who benefits from AI and who falls behind.

3. Data Literacy in the Age of AI

As people lean on AI to analyze data, are they getting better or worse at understanding and questioning it? Expectations are rising faster than skills.

How AI reliance affects critical thinking

A Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey of 319 knowledge workers (936 real tasks) found:

  • More confidence in AI was linked to less critical thinking.
  • More confidence in one's own skills was linked to more critical thinking.
  • Workers' effort shifts from analyzing information to verifying AI output.

Self-reported survey; shows association, not cause.

AI use vs. AI training at work

Share of employees

What you're looking at: Almost everyone uses AI at work, but only a third were trained by their employer. Nearly half taught themselves, so many people rely on AI without formal guidance on when to trust or question it.

Source: Study.com survey, January 2026.

Key takeaway: Reliance on AI is growing faster than people's ability to question it. That is a risk for data-driven decisions, because a decision is only as good as the human judgment that checks the analysis.

Methodology

  • Source mix: evidence comes from government (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Census, Federal Reserve), academic (Stanford, Yale, MIT, Carnegie Mellon, University of Washington) and industry sources (McKinsey, PwC, WEF, DataCamp).
  • Both sides shown: each finding was sorted as a positive finding or a concern before drawing conclusions, and each section ends with a key takeaway.
  • Conflicts of interest flagged: consultancies and training vendors sell AI services, so their findings are paired with independent data.
  • Transparent sourcing: every number is named with its source, listed in full below.
  • Known limits: many figures are self-reported surveys; layoffs "citing" AI may reflect other causes; the Census changed its AI question in late 2025, so older and newer rates aren't directly comparable.
